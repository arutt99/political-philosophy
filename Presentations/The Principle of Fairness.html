Political obligation

The Principle of Fairness

Can the benefits of social cooperation create a duty to obey?

Central problem: whether fair reciprocity can ground political obligation when actual consent is absent.

SECTION 01

From consent to fair contribution

Hart’s principle shifts the basis of obligation from an unspoken promise to reciprocity within a cooperative practice.

The starting problem

Consent fails as a description

Hume challenges the claim that political ties rest on “voluntary consent or a mutual promise.”

Most people inherit political membership. They never make a free promise to obey.

Fairness begins from a different fact: people can benefit from cooperation without promising anything.

Citation: David Hume, “Of the Original Contract” (1748), p. 470.

Fair reciprocity

The core fairness claim

1

Other people accept restrictions that sustain a cooperative practice.

2

Their compliance creates benefits that you enjoy.

3

Fairness requires comparable submission when the rules call for your share.

Taking the benefit while refusing the burden can exploit the people who cooperate.

Citation: H. L. A. Hart, “Are There Any Natural Rights?” (1955), p. 85.

H. L. A. Hart

Hart’s principle

1

People conduct a joint enterprise according to rules.

2

Following those rules restricts the liberty of the participants.

3

Other people benefit from the participants’ submission.

4

The participants may demand similar submission from those beneficiaries.

Citation: Hart, “Are There Any Natural Rights?” p. 85.

Two grounds of obligation

The “rational core” of tacit consent

Tacit consent

Receiving benefits counts as an unspoken promise to obey.

Fairness

Receiving benefits matters because taking them without sharing the costs can exploit other people.

The alleged duty comes from reciprocity rather than a promise.

Hart calls fairness the “rational core” of tacit consent. The phrase preserves the anti-exploitation idea without treating benefit as an act of agreement.

The political application

Benefits and political burdens

Benefit

Security and stability

Enforced laws protect people within a dependable social order.

Burden

Political obligations

People obey laws and contribute taxes that keep the legal order working.

A beneficiary should not claim the security while making everyone else carry its costs.

The benefit-burden link supplies the anti-free-rider force of the principle.

Case facts

The round of drinks

  1. Four friends follow a practice in which each person buys a round.
  2. Three friends each buy drinks for all four people.
  3. The fourth friend leaves after the third drink, just before buying a round.

Decision at issue: whether accepting the three drinks makes the fourth friend responsible for a comparable contribution.

The example illustrates an accepted cooperative practice with recognizable benefits and costs.

Case assessment

Why leaving looks unfair

1

The fourth friend participates in the round-buying practice.

2

The friend accepts benefits produced by the others’ contributions.

3

Leaving shifts the expected burden back onto those who already paid.

The conduct looks like free riding because the friend accepted the practice and then avoided its cost.

The example supports obligations within a scheme that the beneficiary appears to accept. Later cases test whether receipt alone can do the same work.

Conditional argument

The political inference

1

The legal order gives every person security and stability.

2

Those benefits depend on widespread compliance and contribution.

3

Fairness requires each beneficiary to carry a comparable share.

If every person benefits, each person appears to acquire political obligations.

The universal conclusion depends on the premise that every person receives a qualifying benefit.

A necessary premise

The universal-benefit premise

Fairness can ground a universal duty only if the state provides the relevant benefit to every person it claims to bind.

Hobbes

The dangers outside common authority can support the claim that civil order benefits people.

Hume

Enforced justice serves each person’s interest by preserving a stable practice that voluntary restraint cannot sustain.

Hume supplies the detailed benefit argument used in the next section. He is not himself committed to the fairness theory.

SECTION 02

Why government benefits us

Hume explains the advantages of enforced justice by connecting institutional design with a weakness in human motivation.

Hume’s first step

Justice serves each person’s long-term interest

1

Rules of justice protect private property and personal security.

2

Compliance sometimes requires a person to surrender an immediate advantage.

3

Stable protection over time benefits that same person more than a particular violation would.

Justice pays in the long term, but only while people generally follow its rules.

Hume’s argument targets each person’s interest, not only aggregate social welfare.

An apparent contradiction

The enforcement puzzle

Claim

Obedience serves each person’s interest.

Observed practice

Government threatens punishment to secure obedience.

If people already gain from obeying, the need for force demands an explanation.

Hume resolves the puzzle by separating a person’s considered interest from the motive that governs a present choice.

Human motivation

Two time horizons

Near

A small gain from breaking a rule arrives now.

Remote

A larger benefit from stable justice arrives over time.

Judgment ranks the remote benefit higher.

Motivation often chooses the near advantage.

Citation: Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, p. 535.

The breakdown mechanism

Individual temptation becomes collective loss

01

One person gains by breaking a rule.

02

Other people pursue the same immediate advantage.

03

General compliance erodes and justice stops protecting anyone reliably.

04

Everyone loses the larger benefit of social order.

Reason identifies the final loss, but the immediate gain can still dominate action.

Hume’s explanation

Reason does not control motivation

Reason

Reason shows that lawful order serves our greater long-term interest.

Passion

Desire gives stronger motivational weight to what feels immediate and close.

Knowledge of our interest cannot reliably sustain justice on its own.

Citation: Hume, Treatise of Human Nature, p. 535. Hume describes reason as “the slave of the passions.”

Institutional response

Changing the choice situation

Institutions must make observance of justice our “nearest interest.”

Without a sanction

Disobedience can offer the strongest immediate payoff.

With a sanction

Punishment attaches a near cost to disobedience, so compliance also serves immediate interest.

Citation: Hume, Treatise of Human Nature, p. 537.

The role of government

Civil magistrates align interests

Long term

Laws preserve order

General compliance maintains the system of justice that benefits everyone over time.

Short term

Enforcement changes incentives

Credible punishment gives each person an immediate reason to comply.

Coercion helps people act in accordance with their own rational interest.

Hume argues that people can reasonably welcome enforcement because it stabilizes a mutually beneficial practice.

A limit on the argument

Hume’s conclusion stops at advantage

Government can protect everyone’s interest even when political authority does not rest on consent.

Established

Government provides advantages and people have reason to accept its enforcement.

Still unproven

Every person has a moral obligation to obey the state.

Usefulness and moral authority remain separate claims. Hume does not supply the further moral premise.

The further step

Fairness adds the moral bridge

1

Government supplies benefits through enforced justice.

2

Other citizens bear restrictions and costs that help produce those benefits.

3

A beneficiary who refuses comparable burdens treats those citizens unfairly.

The fairness theorist converts governmental advantage into a claimed duty to obey.

Hume supplies the benefit premise. Hart’s principle supplies the moral requirement of reciprocal contribution.

Section recap

The argument so far

Enforced justice protects each person’s long-term interest.

Government makes compliance attractive in the short term as well.

Fairness then claims that beneficiaries owe the contributions that sustain those advantages.

The next objection targets the move from receiving a benefit to owing a contribution.

SECTION 03

Unsolicited benefits

Nozick challenges the assumption that receipt of a benefit automatically creates a debt.

A changed case

The refused round

  1. Before anyone buys drinks, one person says they will not buy a round.
  2. The others still supply that person with three drinks.
  3. They later demand a fourth round in return.

Decision at issue: whether benefits supplied after an explicit refusal can create a duty to repay.

The altered facts remove the appearance that the beneficiary accepted the cooperative practice.

Robert Nozick

Nozick’s principle

Unsolicited benefits do not automatically create obligations to reciprocate.

A provider cannot create another person’s debt merely by supplying something that person never requested.

Enjoyment may show that the benefit had value. It does not show authorization of the scheme or its price.

Citation: Robert Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974), p. 93.

Case facts

The public-address-system case

  1. Neighbors create a shared entertainment system.
  2. Each resident receives an assigned day to broadcast music or jokes.
  3. You hear and enjoy 137 days of other people’s broadcasts.
  4. Day 138 demands that you give up a day to entertain everyone else.

Decision at issue: whether enjoyment alone makes the assigned labor morally obligatory.

Citation: Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, p. 93.

Case assessment

Enjoyment alone creates no duty

1

You never asked the neighbors to organize the broadcasts.

2

The benefit reached you whether you wanted the scheme or not.

3

Hearing or enjoying a broadcast does not commit you to its assigned cost.

Hart’s receipt-based principle appears to demand a contribution that Nozick regards as unjustified.

The case separates benefit, enjoyment, and acceptance. They do not necessarily occur together.

The general objection

Receipt-based duties give providers too much power

01

A provider chooses what counts as a benefit.

02

The provider supplies it without permission.

03

The provider sets the required repayment.

04

The provider calls refusal unfair.

A theory of fairness cannot let one side create another person’s debt unilaterally.

Nozick’s objection does not deny every obligation of reciprocity. It denies that mere receipt can generate one.

SECTION 04

The acceptance revision

The proposed revision replaces receipt with informed acceptance. That repair improves the principle but narrows its reach.

The proposed revision

Acceptance rather than receipt

Mere receipt

A benefit reaches you regardless of your choice.

No obligation follows automatically.

Acceptance

You take the benefit as part of a cooperative scheme while understanding its costs.

Reciprocal contribution can become obligatory.
The revision targets a genuine free rider rather than a passive recipient.

The distinction preserves the anti-exploitation intuition while blocking duties created solely by imposed goods.

The revision applied

The public-address case after the repair

1

Merely hearing the broadcasts creates no duty.

2

Accepting the scheme as a whole includes accepting an assigned day of contribution.

3

Taking the scheme’s benefits and then evading its understood cost constitutes free riding.

Enforcement can answer exploitation when acceptance is genuine and the costs were understood.

The revision creates plausible obligations inside accepted cooperative schemes.

A new difficulty

State benefits are difficult to accept voluntarily

Legal protection and public order reach people whether they request them or not.

Practical problem

Most people cannot reject the central benefits while remaining within the state’s territory.

Conceptual problem

If unavoidable residence or use counts as acceptance, the theory makes acceptance automatic.

Automatic acceptance recreates the weakness of tacit consent.

The revision needs a meaningful distinction between accepted benefits and benefits that merely arrive.

The cost of genuine acceptance

Genuine refusal limits the principle

1

Anarchists may understand the advantages of government.

2

They may still reject the state’s scheme because they judge its other costs unacceptable.

3

If acceptance grounds the duty, a genuine refuser acquires no fairness obligation.

A plausible acceptance requirement prevents the principle from binding everyone.

Acknowledging that government offers benefits does not amount to accepting the full cooperative arrangement.

Two versions

The final dilemma

Receipt-based fairness

It can reach every recipient, including people who never requested the benefits.

Broad enough for universality, but vulnerable to Nozick’s objection.

Acceptance-based fairness

It binds people who knowingly join the scheme and understand its costs.

Morally stronger, but unable to bind genuine refusers.
The principle cannot secure both a plausible basis of obligation and a universal duty to obey.

The dilemma identifies the tradeoff between moral plausibility and universal reach.

Conclusion

What the principle establishes

Fairness identifies a real wrong: exploiting a cooperative practice that one genuinely accepts.

Mere receipt reaches more people, but imposed benefits cannot create debts by themselves.

Requiring acceptance protects recipients, but some people can refuse the political scheme.

The principle improves on consent theory but does not establish a universal political obligation.

The limited success is important. The theory explains duties inside accepted schemes even though it cannot justify a duty for every person under a state.

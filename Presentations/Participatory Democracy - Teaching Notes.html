Participatory Democracy Teaching Notes

A guide to the 42-slide presentation on citizen influence, public voice, and practical democratic institutions.

The central lesson is that participation should make authority answerable to the people affected by it. Broader involvement, careful discussion, and real decision power are connected aims, each with its own difficulties.

The modern extensions focus on Pateman and Barber’s case for participation; Habermas on public justification; Young and Fraser on exclusion; Fung and Wright on institutional authority; and Fishkin and Ackerman on democratic experiments.

SLIDE 01Open this slide

Participatory Democracy

People should have a meaningful say in the decisions that shape their lives.

Teaching purpose. Introduce the central claim and the lesson’s practical question.

Context. This lecture develops a demanding ideal of self-rule, then tests how institutions might make it usable. Participation can mean speaking, proposing, choosing, or reviewing decisions; those powers need to be distinguished.

Teaching approach. Open with a familiar experience: a rule at work, at college, or in a neighborhood that affected students but gave them little influence. Invite two brief examples, then connect them to the claim on the slide.

Distinction to preserve. The modern thinkers differ in their aims. Pateman and Barber defend broad participation; Habermas and Fishkin develop deliberative approaches; Young and Fraser expose barriers to equal voice; Fung and Wright connect participation to government authority.

Next connection. Explain the appeal of self-rule before considering any particular reform.

SLIDE 02Open this slide

A say in the rules can support freedom

When people help shape rules they must obey, political authority can become more answerable to them.

Participatory democracy extends this ideal through continuing involvement in public decisions.

Teaching purpose. State the strongest rationale for participation.

Context. The argument connects freedom with influence over the terms of shared life. An election gives citizens one kind of influence; ongoing participation promises additional opportunities to question and revise decisions.

Teaching approach. Contrast two identical college policies: students had no input into one; they helped draft and review the other. Ask why the procedure could matter even when the final policy is the same.

Discussion prompt. What changes when those affected can help revise a rule?

A useful response. They can expose overlooked burdens and make decision makers explain themselves. A fair procedure can matter separately from whether someone likes the outcome.

Distinction to preserve. This is an argument for democratic authority, not proof that every participant personally consents to every rule.

Next connection. Define participation and deliberation separately.

SLIDE 03Open this slide

Participation and deliberation

Participation: People take part in shaping decisions. The issue is how much influence they exercise.

Deliberation: People weigh information and reasons together. The issue is how carefully they reach judgments.

Teaching purpose. Make the relationship between the two traditions precise.

Context. An open vote may involve many people with little discussion. A carefully moderated forum may involve only a small sample. A participatory budget can combine both, depending on its design.

Teaching approach. Give the class two examples: a poll sent to every resident and a weekend discussion among fifty selected residents. Ask which expands involvement and which creates space for considered judgment.

Distinction to preserve. The categories overlap without being synonyms. Representation can also coexist with both. A citizen forum is not automatically a direct democracy or a legislature.

Next connection. A meaningful opportunity to participate must include disagreement.

SLIDE 04Open this slide

Dissent belongs inside self-rule

Citizens need room to challenge a decision and explain whose interests it overlooks.

Losing a vote can leave people with good reasons to keep speaking and seek change.

Teaching purpose. Explain why disagreement can improve democratic judgment.

Context. Respect for dissent changes what self-rule demands: citizens need continuing opportunities to contest decisions, not a duty to adopt the majority’s opinion.

Teaching approach. Use a library closing time that works for most students but excludes commuters. The majority’s preference leaves an identifiable burden that later discussion can uncover.

Discussion prompt. Could a vote be fair while its result still needs revision?

A useful response. Yes. A decision can follow a fair process yet rely on incomplete information or distribute burdens badly.

Distinction to preserve. Preserving dissent does not by itself settle every question about obeying law or civil disobedience.

Next connection. Participation can concern concrete administrative choices as well as laws.

SLIDE 05Open this slide

Public participation can reach administration

A law can authorize a service while officials decide how that service actually works.

Citizens might help choose a bus route or a local spending priority because these decisions shape everyday life.

Teaching purpose. Extend democratic influence beyond general legislation.

Context. The distinction between general rules and particular acts matters because people experience many consequences through implementation. Public input need not stop when a legislature passes a law.

Teaching approach. A council approves public transit. Administrators still choose routes and operating hours. Ask which stage matters most to someone returning home after a late shift.

Distinction to preserve. The point supports participation in important administrative decisions. It does not require a public vote on every operational detail.

Next connection. Consider authority outside government as well.

SLIDE 06Open this slide

Democracy can reach everyday institutions

At work, decisions about schedules and conditions affect people who may have little control over them.

In households and other shared institutions, democratic equality also asks whose voice carries weight.

Teaching purpose. Explain the proposal that democracy should reach throughout social life.

Context. Equal voting rights can coexist with unequal authority in daily relationships. The participatory ideal asks whether people can influence decisions wherever they regularly live under someone else’s power.

Teaching approach. Invite students to distinguish influence over a household’s shared responsibilities from voting over a person’s private choices. The first concerns shared authority; the second can violate autonomy.

Distinction to preserve. Extending democratic principles does not make every personal decision a matter for majority rule. Protecting individual freedom remains necessary.

Next connection. Bring out the ideal’s strong claim about obligation, then its limit.

SLIDE 07Open this slide

Participation and political obligation

A strong participatory view holds that genuine involvement makes citizens contributing members of a shared political order.

But a chance to influence a decision does not show that each person agreed to the final result.

Teaching purpose. Separate a rationale for authority from proof of individual consent.

Context. The demanding version links a participatory society with an obligation to obey. Its appeal is that people become more than passive recipients of rules. The second statement tests the move from influence to consent.

Teaching approach. Suppose a student helps design a college policy but votes against its final version. Their contribution may support the procedure’s legitimacy while leaving their disagreement intact.

Discussion prompt. What else would a fair duty to comply require?

A useful response. Possible answers include equal standing, rights, a fair process, or reciprocal obligations. Students should name the additional premise instead of treating participation as automatic consent.

Distinction to preserve. Present the strong thesis as a contested view. The lecture does not establish a general obligation to obey every democratic decision.

Next connection. Introduce a concrete case that will recur through the modern thinkers.

SLIDE 08Open this slide

Riverside must choose a project

The council has $300,000. Safer bus stops and a park renovation each cost the full amount.

Officials plan a public meeting at 6 p.m. Residents must decide which project to support.

Teaching purpose. Give the facts and the decision before applying theories.

Context. All Riverside facts are invented for classroom analysis. Keep the cost equal so that arguments concern needs and democratic procedure rather than arithmetic. Later slides add clearly stated details.

Teaching approach. Ask for an initial show of hands after stating the facts. Record reasons without declaring a winner. Explain that the class will revisit both the choice and the process used to make it.

Distinction to preserve. Do not present Riverside as a real city, research finding, or account of any thinker’s own example. Some students may correctly ask to revise the two-option agenda; preserve that observation for later.

Next connection. Pateman asks what people can learn when they exercise real influence.

SLIDE 09Open this slide

People learn by taking part

Pateman argues that exercising influence can develop the skills and confidence needed for democratic participation.

Institutions can help create capable citizens by giving them regular opportunities to make decisions.

Teaching purpose. Explain Pateman’s account of learning through participation.

Context. Participation and Democratic Theory (1970) challenged theories that treated ordinary citizens’ limited engagement as a reason to keep their role small. Pateman’s later account restates the relationship between capacities and democratic authority.

Teaching approach. Use the first attempt to run a student society meeting: participants may learn how to propose an agenda, listen, and revise a plan through practice. Compare that with receiving rules someone else has already set.

Distinction to preserve. This is a developmental argument, not a claim that every meeting improves everyone. Good opportunities and genuine influence matter.

Next connection. Identify what distinguishes an opportunity to speak from a share in authority.

SLIDE 10Open this slide

Consultation can leave power untouched

Advice only: Workers suggest changes to their schedule, but the manager keeps complete discretion.

A share in authority: Workers help decide the scheduling rules and can challenge departures from them.

Teaching purpose. Distinguish being heard from having an institutional share in decisions.

Context. Pateman’s participatory society involves changing undemocratic authority structures. A participatory label is insufficient if existing decision makers retain all the power.

Teaching approach. Ask students to identify the rule that would have to change in the workplace example. Encourage specific answers: agreed decision rights, an appeal, or a binding procedure.

Discussion prompt. What evidence would show that the workers’ influence is real?

A useful response. Look for a defined power to shape rules or outcomes, rather than a promise that a manager will listen.

Distinction to preserve. Advice can still be useful. The comparison identifies a difference in authority, not a claim that every consultation has no value.

Next connection. Test the limits of the learning argument.

SLIDE 11Open this slide

Participation needs conditions that support learning

A meeting dominated by experienced speakers may give newcomers little chance to practice.

Pateman’s argument gives us a reason to examine how institutions share time and responsibility.

Teaching purpose. Apply a plausible objection to the developmental argument.

Context. This is a classroom inference from the learning argument. A busy institution can invite attendance while offering participants little responsibility. The resulting experience could discourage participation.

Teaching approach. Return to Riverside. Ask whether repeatedly attending a meeting without ever affecting its outcome would build confidence. Have students propose one change that would let newcomers contribute.

Distinction to preserve. Low participation can have several causes. Do not infer that exclusion explains every case, or that participation reliably produces greater political competence without evidence.

Next connection. Barber gives a further reason to participate: public choices can change how people understand their interests.

SLIDE 12Open this slide

Strong democracy develops shared judgment

Barber’s “strong democracy” gives citizens recurring roles in deciding important public questions.

Working through conflict together can change what people understand their common interests to be.

Teaching purpose. Define strong democracy and explain its distinctive claim.

Context. Strong Democracy (1984) makes participation part of citizenship. Barber allows citizens to govern at some levels and at some times; the ideal does not demand constant involvement in every decision.

Teaching approach. Contrast a list of fixed preferences with a conversation in which people discover a shared problem. Keep the distinction focused on whether preferences can change through public work.

Distinction to preserve. Barber is not simply asking for more frequent opinion polls. His claim concerns the formation of public purposes through ongoing participation.

Next connection. Apply the claim to the budget case.

SLIDE 13Open this slide

Discussion can change Riverside’s priorities

Park supporters and bus users initially defend the project each would use.

After discussion, they might agree that people’s ability to reach public spaces safely deserves priority.

Teaching purpose. Illustrate how participation could reshape a public purpose.

Context. This is an interpretation of Barber applied to an invented case. It proposes one possible change in reasoning, not a predicted empirical result.

Teaching approach. Ask the class to describe a reason for changing a preference that differs from simply losing a vote. A park supporter might still enjoy the park while giving priority to another group’s more urgent need.

Discussion prompt. What would make a changed view thoughtful rather than pressured?

A useful response. The person should be able to explain the evidence or reason that changed their assessment and remain free to disagree.

Distinction to preserve. Neither agreement nor choosing bus stops is required by Barber’s theory in this example.

Next connection. A shared purpose can also conceal pressure to conform.

SLIDE 14Open this slide

A shared purpose can silence a minority

A group may describe its preferred project as what “the whole community” wants.

A democratic process still needs room for people to reject that description and argue for another priority.

Teaching purpose. State a risk of appeals to community and the consequence for dissent.

Context. Barber distinguishes strong democracy from a coercive unity that erases differences. The objection here tests how a real institution preserves that distinction.

Teaching approach. Imagine a chair saying that continued opposition shows disloyalty to Riverside. Ask students what the chair has substituted for an answer to the objection.

Distinction to preserve. This is a pressure problem, not proof that common purposes are impossible. Appeals to community can support cooperation while still requiring criticism.

Next connection. Habermas places justification at the center of democratic judgment.

SLIDE 15Open this slide

Public decisions need reasons others can question

Habermas argues that public discussion helps justify a government’s right to make decisions.

The aim is to give reasons weight without allowing wealth or status to settle the issue.

Teaching purpose. Explain the deliberative ideal of public justification.

Context. Three Normative Models of Democracy (1994) and Between Facts and Norms (English edition, 1996) connect public discussion with constitutional procedures. They concern the conditions under which political authority can be justified.

Teaching approach. Give two explanations for the same spending choice: a donor demanded it; residents offered reasons about its effects. Ask what the second explanation permits other citizens to do.

Distinction to preserve. A persuasive speaker can still be wrong. The ideal concerns an open process of justification, not a guarantee that the most polished argument wins or that everyone reaches agreement.

Next connection. Show what public justification asks of the council in Riverside.

SLIDE 16Open this slide

Riverside must explain its choice

The council should explain the benefits and burdens of each project and allow residents to challenge its evidence.

A decision deserves stronger support when officials can answer objections from people affected by it.

Teaching purpose. Apply public justification to a concrete decision.

Context. This is an instructional application, not Habermas’s own case. Ask whether the council can defend the procedure and the reasons it used, even to residents whose favored project loses.

Teaching approach. Let students improve a weak justification such as “more people liked the park.” They may ask who would use each project, whose needs are urgent, and what alternatives staff excluded.

Discussion prompt. What question would you ask before accepting the council’s reasons?

A useful response. Good answers identify missing information or an overlooked burden, rather than only restating a preference.

Distinction to preserve. Public reasons can be disputed after the vote. A defensible procedure does not require every person to endorse the result.

Next connection. Deliberation can work through institutions with different responsibilities.

SLIDE 17Open this slide

Discussion and government can have different roles

Public debate can identify problems and put pressure on elected institutions.

Formal institutions still make and implement decisions under procedures that protect rights.

Teaching purpose. Explain how deliberation can coexist with representative government.

Context. Habermas links informal public discussion with organized decision procedures. His account does not require the whole citizenry to act as one body making every decision.

Teaching approach. Use three ordinary roles without turning them into a hierarchy of worth: residents raise a neglected issue; elected officials authorize a policy; staff deliver it. Ask where accountability must connect those roles.

Distinction to preserve. Avoid presenting Habermas as an advocate of replacing legislatures with one continuous town meeting. His framework also takes rights and constitutional procedures seriously.

Next connection. Young asks whether an apparently open discussion actually hears everyone.

SLIDE 18Open this slide

Equal invitations can still produce unequal voice

Young argues that rules of public discussion can favor people trained to speak in formal arguments.

People can enter the room and still find that others dismiss their experience or manner of speaking.

Teaching purpose. Define exclusion within an apparently inclusive forum.

Context. Inclusion and Democracy (2000), especially chapter 2, expands political communication to include acknowledgment, narrative, and rhetoric alongside argument. The concern is unequal credibility as well as formal access.

Teaching approach. Compare a confident expert and a nervous resident describing the same problem. Ask students to distinguish relevance from polish before anyone assesses the speaker’s credibility.

Distinction to preserve. Young does not abandon argument or claim that every statement deserves equal evidential weight. Her account asks what communication helps people understand one another.

Next connection. Give a concrete example of relevant experience expressed through a story.

SLIDE 19Open this slide

A story can reveal what the figures miss

A Riverside cleaner describes waiting alone at an unlit bus stop after a late shift.

That experience gives the group a reason to ask whose safety the project comparisons have overlooked.

Teaching purpose. Show how narrative can contribute to judgment.

Context. The cleaner and the unlit stop are additional hypothetical details. The point is that experience can identify a relevant question that an official summary did not raise.

Teaching approach. Ask students to turn the story into a question the council should investigate. Examples: how many people use the stop after dark, what hazards they encounter, and whether the proposed project addresses them.

Discussion prompt. Does hearing this story settle the budget choice?

A useful response. No. It identifies a concern and directs inquiry. Other experiences and evidence still matter.

Distinction to preserve. Neither dismiss the account as merely emotional nor treat one person’s account as proof of how everyone experiences transit.

Next connection. Participation can also happen outside a formal meeting.

SLIDE 20Open this slide

Protest can bring an excluded issue into debate

Residents may use a demonstration or public art to make a neglected problem visible.

Such action can widen democratic attention, while its claims remain open to criticism.

Teaching purpose. Explain the democratic role of communication beyond formal debate.

Context. Young’s account includes protest within a broader picture of political communication. Her 2001 essay also frames tensions between activism and deliberative norms; it is an extension for further study.

Teaching approach. Imagine bus users gathering outside the meeting with pictures of unsafe stops. Ask what their action can achieve before a vote occurs.

Distinction to preserve. Protest is not automatically truthful or inclusive. Evaluate what it reveals, how it affects other people, and whether its claims can be tested. The lecture does not establish that every disruptive act is justified.

Next connection. Fraser considers why marginalized people sometimes need spaces of their own.

SLIDE 21Open this slide

Separate forums can widen public debate

Fraser argues that disadvantaged groups may need their own spaces to describe shared problems and develop a public voice.

Those spaces can help people challenge the terms of a wider debate.

Teaching purpose. Explain why one common forum may be insufficient.

Context. Fraser calls these alternative arenas “subaltern counterpublics.” Translate the term immediately as spaces where marginalized groups develop and circulate their own accounts of their needs. Formal openness does not erase unequal power. Her essay critiques the earlier model of the public sphere associated with Habermas; it predates his later constitutional theory.

Teaching approach. An invented night-shift workers’ group compares experiences before meeting officials. Ask what members could learn there that might be hard to say in a forum dominated by employers.

Distinction to preserve. A separate forum is a possible response to exclusion, not a guarantee of justice or an ideal of permanent isolation.

Next connection. Use the idea to revisit who chose Riverside’s options.

SLIDE 22Open this slide

Public voice includes challenging the agenda

Riverside’s night workers may discover that the deepest problem is the lack of late bus service.

They can challenge an agenda that offers only bus-stop improvements or a park renovation.

Teaching purpose. Show how participation can change the question itself.

Context. The workers’ group and missing service are hypothetical. Their discussion exposes how an apparently inclusive vote can exclude a relevant option before anyone casts a ballot.

Teaching approach. Ask students to explain why the initial cost constraint cannot simply settle the new proposal. The council needs to explain whether late service is feasible and whether the spending agenda can change.

Distinction to preserve. Fraser’s separate forums should be able to communicate with the wider public. They can contain their own inequalities; a group’s account also needs scrutiny.

Next connection. Fung and Wright ask how discussion can carry public authority.

SLIDE 23Open this slide

Participation can carry public authority

Fung and Wright study institutions in which affected people and officials work on concrete public problems.

Their “empowered participatory governance” gives these groups a role in decisions that guide action.

Teaching purpose. Define the institutional proposal and its distinctive feature.

Context. Deepening Democracy (2003) studies experiments rather than proposing one universal assembly. The central concern is connecting practical discussion with decision power. Translate “empowered” as having a defined authority to affect what government does.

Teaching approach. Ask students what would change if Riverside’s residents could choose a funded project under published rules rather than offer advice at an optional hearing.

Distinction to preserve. Do not equate every participatory meeting with this model. Actual authority and links to wider public institutions are essential.

Next connection. Implementation still needs people with assigned responsibilities.

SLIDE 24Open this slide

Citizen decisions still need implementation

Citizens can choose priorities while staff work out technical details and carry out the plan.

Shared control requires a way to check whether those responsible actually deliver it.

Teaching purpose. Explain why participation and delegation can coexist.

Context. The difficulty is the relation between choosing and acting. A collective decision does not pave a road, prepare a contract, or maintain a service by itself.

Teaching approach. For safer bus stops, ask who checks land access, lighting specifications, and maintenance costs. Then ask who reviews a delay or unexplained change.

Distinction to preserve. Technical skill matters, but expertise does not automatically settle priorities. Staff should explain feasibility constraints in language participants can examine.

Next connection. Local decisions also need connections to institutions beyond the neighborhood.

SLIDE 25Open this slide

Local control needs wider public oversight

A local group may overlook people outside its boundaries or favor its most organized members.

Fung and Wright connect local decision powers with coordination and accountability beyond the group.

Teaching purpose. Explain a limit of local participation and the institutional response.

Context. The design includes local authority alongside links for resources, responsibility, and review. Participation can reproduce power inequalities if better organized groups dominate.

Teaching approach. Suppose one neighborhood’s preferred traffic change sends heavy vehicles into another neighborhood. Ask why local agreement is insufficient.

Distinction to preserve. Oversight can protect outsiders but can also weaken local influence. Have students explain who reviews decisions and what reasons justify intervention.

Next connection. Examine a historical budgeting practice with both local participation and government institutions.

SLIDE 26Open this slide

Participatory budgeting in Porto Alegre

Residents in Porto Alegre, Brazil, helped set public spending priorities through meetings and delegate structures.

Their involvement reached allocations of public money, giving discussion a connection to government action.

Teaching purpose. Introduce a real institutional example without overstating its scope.

Context. Participatory budgeting began there in 1989. Citizens discussed investment priorities within a municipal process; they did not collectively run every operation or decide the entire municipal budget.

Teaching approach. Compare the historical mechanism with Riverside’s optional meeting. Ask which decisions each process actually empowers participants to shape.

Distinction to preserve. This is a historical illustration. It makes no claim about Porto Alegre’s current process or that participation alone caused all reported social improvements.

Next connection. The budget’s size and the rules around it determine the reach of participation.

SLIDE 27Open this slide

A small budget gives citizens limited control

Residents can control a neighborhood fund while officials retain power over much larger spending choices.

To assess participation, we need to know which decisions citizens can change and which decisions remain elsewhere.

Teaching purpose. Explain why a participatory label does not establish broad influence.

Context. Fung’s 2006 account examines who participates, how they communicate, and the authority connected to the process. This slide applies the authority dimension to a hypothetical small fund.

Teaching approach. Suppose Riverside invites residents to choose benches but excludes the bus timetable. Ask whether this process addresses the problem raised by night workers.

Distinction to preserve. A narrow process may still be useful. Assess it in relation to its stated purpose and the needs it can actually affect.

Next connection. Fishkin tackles another difficulty: giving careful discussion time to a manageable group.

SLIDE 28Open this slide

A small sample can have time to think

Fishkin’s deliberative polling brings a randomly selected sample of citizens into structured discussion.

It explores what public opinion might look like when people have time and information to examine the choices.

Teaching purpose. Explain the rationale for a deliberative poll.

Context. The method gives participants an opportunity to consider trade-offs rather than answer immediately. Sampling aims to include a range of people, while moderated discussion makes a large population’s problem manageable.

Teaching approach. Ask why an open meeting and a selected sample might attract different people. Keep sampling distinct from electing representatives.

Distinction to preserve. A sample’s judgments are evidence about considered opinion. They do not automatically create a right to make binding decisions for everyone.

Next connection. Show the actual steps in the method.

SLIDE 29Open this slide

Deliberative polling compares judgments over time

Before: A sampled group answers questions about the issue.

During: Participants examine balanced materials and discuss questions with one another and competing experts.

After: They answer the original questions again, privately. Agreement is not required.

Teaching purpose. Explain how the method produces its evidence.

Context. The before-and-after design measures changes in individual judgments. Confidential questionnaires reduce pressure to endorse a single group answer. Bigger groups do not automatically deliberate better: more speakers can make it harder to follow an argument or give each participant a turn.

Teaching approach. Reveal each step separately. Ask what the final private questionnaire preserves that a public show of hands might obscure.

Distinction to preserve. The organizers’ choice of topics and materials still matters. An opinion change does not by itself demonstrate that a decision is correct.

Next connection. Consider a documented experiment and the limits of its evidence.

SLIDE 30Open this slide

America in One Room tested this approach

526 registered voters gathered for a national deliberative poll about major policy choices.

Republican and Democratic views moved closer on 22 of 26 proposals that initially sharply divided the parties.

Teaching purpose. Give a concrete research finding with a clear comparison.

Context. Fishkin, Siu, Diamond, and Bradburn published their analysis in 2021. The event recruited a sample through NORC and also used a comparison group of 844 registered voters who did not receive the same deliberative intervention.

Teaching approach. The unit of the result is a policy proposal, not a person. Ask students to explain “22 of 26” accurately before considering what it suggests.

Distinction to preserve. The design bundled information, discussion, facilitation, and contact with other participants. The result supports this organized process under these conditions; it does not show that all discussion reduces polarization. Movement toward agreement is also not proof of truth. Not every reported movement was statistically significant.

A thoughtful sample leaves a participation question

A sampled forum can produce considered judgments while most citizens take no part in its discussion.

Officials must explain how those judgments will influence decisions and how others can challenge them.

Teaching purpose. Separate thoughtful discussion from broad democratic control.

Context. This compares the deliberative poll’s purpose with Pateman’s demand for wider changes in authority. The proposals solve different problems and can be combined.

Teaching approach. Suppose Riverside commissions an excellent poll but ignores it. Then suppose the council makes the poll binding without further public explanation. Ask what democratic concern appears in each case.

Distinction to preserve. A deliberative poll is often advisory. A citizens’ assembly can have a different remit and may issue collective recommendations; the terms should not be used interchangeably.

Participation takes time people may not have

Riverside needs a fair decision process

The council’s $300,000 choice affects residents who disagree and have unequal time to participate.

Design a process that lets people propose options, contribute relevant experience, and see how their input affects the decision.

Teaching purpose. Use the concepts to design an institution rather than only name thinkers.

Context. Give groups four minutes. Each group should specify one rule for agenda proposals, one way to reach people missing from the meeting, and one defined decision power. Ask them to name a trade-off their design leaves unresolved.

Teaching approach. Keep the facts visible. Clarify that proposing additional options requires a feasibility check; the exercise does not give the city an unlimited budget.

Discussion prompt. Which rule in your design most directly changes who has power?

A useful response. Students should point to a specific authority, such as placing an item on the agenda or choosing among feasible funded projects.

Distinction to preserve. There is no single prescribed theory-consistent winner. Evaluate the procedure and its remaining weaknesses.

SLIDE 37Open this slide

One possible Riverside design

Agenda: Residents can propose projects; staff publish feasibility reasons and residents can appeal exclusions.

Decision: Accessible discussion informs a resident vote among feasible projects within the fund.

Review: Staff publish progress, and residents can challenge unexplained delays or changes.

Teaching purpose. Show how the theories can inform a concrete procedure.

Context. This is the instructor’s synthesis of several concerns, not a plan endorsed jointly by the thinkers. In discussion, add paid participation time, childcare, and outreach to people who use the services but may not attend.

Teaching approach. Reveal each stage and identify the problem it addresses. Then ask what happens to people who lack legal residency, cannot use the voting platform, or bear spillover costs.

Distinction to preserve. The design remains limited to a specified fund and electorate. It still needs rights protections, selection rules, and procedures for revising decisions.

Voice and authority need separate tests

Voice: Habermas, Young, and Fraser help us ask whose reasons and experiences the public hears.

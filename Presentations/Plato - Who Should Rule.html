Who Should Rule?

Plato Against Democracy

Is ruling a craft that belongs only to trained experts?

Political Philosophy

The session follows one question through five stages: Plato’s craft analogy, objections to guardianship, the link between voting and interests, Condorcet’s argument about majorities, and the intrinsic value of democracy.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 1–14.

Plato’s allegory · the facts

A ship where no one knows how to steer

01The captain is larger and stronger than anyone aboard, but partly deaf, short-sighted, and weak in seamanship.
02The crew fight over the helm. None has studied navigation, and they insist it cannot be taught.
03The winning faction drugs the captain, seizes the ship, and turns the voyage into a drunken pleasure-cruise.
04The true navigator, who studies the seasons, sky, stars, and winds, is dismissed as a useless star-gazer.

Tell the story before interpreting it. The crew also admire whoever helps them control the captain by force or fraud, and they call that person a skilled sailor.

Citation: Plato, Republic 488a–d, as excerpted in “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 1–2.

Reading the allegory

Each figure stands for part of a democracy

The captain→The people. They hold final power but lack the knowledge to use it well.
The crew→Ambitious politicians and Sophists. They win control through persuasion rather than skill.
The cruise→A misgoverned state. It serves whoever controls the people, by force or by fraud.
The navigator→The expert ruler. Democracy ignores the person who actually knows how to govern.

The captain is not a good ruler held back by others. He is the sovereign people: strong, but unable to see or hear well enough to steer.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 2.

What democracy means

Rule by the demos

Demos

Monarchy is rule by the monarch. Democracy is rule by the demos, which in classical Greek can mean “the people” or “the mob.”

On the second reading, democracy is mob rule: government by the unfit. For Plato this insult is only a preliminary to his main argument.

The ambiguity sets the tone, but the serious argument comes next. Students should not treat the insult as the whole case.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 2.

I

Plato’s main argument

The craft analogy

Ruling is a skill, like medicine or navigation, and skills belong to experts.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 2–3.

The guiding case

When you are ill, you consult a doctor

Consult an expert

A doctor has special training in diagnosing illness and choosing a remedy.

Hold a vote

No sensible patient assembles a crowd and asks it to vote on the correct remedy.

The health of the state matters at least as much as the health of one person. Political decisions also require judgment and skill.

Ask students which option they would choose for their own health, then carry the same logic over to the state.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 2.

The argument

The craft analogy

P1Ruling is a skill, like medicine, navigation, or farming.
P2The skill requires special training, and not everyone is naturally capable of acquiring it.
P3Decisions that require a skill should be left to those who possess it.
SOPolitical decisions belong to trained experts. Consulting the people leads to worse results, even disaster.

Keep this structure visible in students’ minds. Every later objection targets one of these premises or the step to the conclusion.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 3.

Why the people decide badly

Persuasion replaces expertise

01When the people decide, the loudest and most confident speakers, the Sophists, sway them.
02Like the deaf, short-sighted captain, the people accept the false reasoning of ambitious politicians.
03The truly skilled are ignored, so the ship of state loses its way and founders.

This slide connects the allegory back to the argument: democracy fails because it rewards persuasive skill rather than governing skill.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 2.

Plato’s alternative

Philosopher-kings

A just society is impossible unless kings become philosophers or philosophers become kings.

Guardians

Plato’s trained ruling class. For them, philosophical training is a necessary qualification to rule, and it means far more than a few years of reading.

The answer can seem flattering to an audience of philosophy students. Note that “philosopher” names a whole life of education, which the next slide lays out.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 2.

Training the guardians

A lifetime of education comes before rule

Early years

Foundations

Literacy, music, mathematics, and military and physical education.

Age 30

Philosophy begins

Five years of philosophical study.

Age 35

Service

Fifteen years of military service. Only those who pass with honour continue.

Age 50

Philosophy and rule

A permanent life of philosophy, interrupted by turns in “the weary business of politics.”

The point is the scale of the training. Ruling is treated as a demanding craft that takes decades to master, and the guardians see politics as a burden rather than a prize.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 2–3.

Where the argument leaves democracy

If ruling is a rare skill, democracy looks irrational

We leave medicine to trained experts and give medical training to those best suited to it. The craft analogy says ruling should work the same way.

A defender of democracy must find a weakness in the craft analogy. Otherwise, rule by the people looks absurd.

Let the argument land at full strength before introducing objections. The rest of the session tests possible replies.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 3.

II

First line of reply

Problems with guardianship

Plato’s system is a form of dictatorship, so the general arguments against dictatorship apply to it.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 3–5.

Expertise is not authority

Advice from experts differs from rule by experts

Taking advice

You may follow a doctor’s advice or consult an architect. You keep the final decision.

Unchecked power

Imagine “doctor’s orders” with the force of law, or architects deciding who lives in which house.

Even if Plato creates genuine expert rulers, it does not follow that they should have the power to run our lives.

The objection is not that we should never defer to experts. It is that giving experts unchecked power invites catastrophe.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 4.

The corruption objection

Who guards the guardians?

Rulers who are skilled at governing may also be skilled at something else: feathering their own nests.

A surprising preference

If rulers are likely to be corrupt, incompetent rulers may be safer. Their corruption would do less damage.

Expertise does not guarantee good motives. It can make bad motives more effective.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 4.

Plato’s safeguard

Guardians may not own private property

Without private property, a ruling family or clique cannot enrich itself at the people’s expense.

The safeguard works only if the ban can be enforced against the very people who hold power.

Plato anticipated the corruption objection. The question is whether his solution can be enforced.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 4.

The opposite difficulty

Why would anyone agree to rule?

01If guardians gain neither wealth nor privilege, ruling offers them little reward.
02As philosophers, they would rather spend their time reading, talking, and thinking.
03Plato’s answer: they rule grudgingly, to avoid being ruled by others, or worse, by all others.

Removing the rewards of power solves one problem and creates another. Plato’s answer is negative: ruling is a necessary duty, not a desirable position.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 4–5.

Two remaining worries

Enforcement and acceptance

Enforcement

If guardians break the property laws, or change them by proper procedure, no one has the power to stop them.

Public scrutiny by an empowered electorate is a more reliable check than education.

Acceptance

Guardians could be chosen early and tested, as generals rise through an army.

But people without philosophical education may be unable to judge who should rule, and may reject the guardians’ claim.

Both worries concern what happens outside the guardians’ own virtue: who checks them, and whether the governed will accept them.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 5.

Recap · guardianship

The objections show discomfort, not a defense of democracy

01Plato’s system cannot guarantee that guardians will resist temptation, and the people may reject their rule.
02Those problems count against Plato’s particular proposal. Another non-democratic system might avoid them.
03The craft analogy still stands: if only a few possess the skill of ruling, leaving decisions to the mob looks absurd.

This is the turning point of the first half. Criticizing guardianship is not the same as defending democracy. A stronger reply must engage the craft analogy directly.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 5.

III

Second line of reply

Knowledge and interests

If no one can have expert knowledge of ruling, Plato’s case against democracy dissolves.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 6–10.

The sceptical reply

Fallible knowledge does not make everyone equal

The sceptic’s claim

No one can be certain of anything. Trusting so-called experts on any subject is self-deception.

The response

With a broken leg, you would still see a doctor. Doctors make serious mistakes, but untrained impostors do even worse.

Denying all expertise amounts to denying that crafts exist. That is too implausible to believe.

Fallibility cuts against certainty, not against the ordinary fact that some people judge better than others.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 6.

Expertise in ruling

Ruling is, in large part, a craft

Good rulers today need knowledge and abilities that are unevenly distributed:

Economics Psychology and human motivation High intelligence A large capacity for work A keen memory Command of detail Skill with people

It is absurd to think that no one could be a better ruler than anyone else.

Even if expert knowledge exists elsewhere, someone might deny it exists for ruling. The list shows why that denial is also hard to believe.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 6–7.

A more promising line

What is a vote for?

Plato’s assumption

A vote only registers an opinion about what is best for the state as a whole. Experts can form that opinion better.

The democratic possibility

If voting does more than express an opinion about the public good, a stronger defense of democracy becomes possible.

Political decisions may differ from asking for a show of hands on amputating a diseased limb. The next slides explore what else a vote might do.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 7.

For the people, by the people

Rule for the people must be rule by the people

Common ground

Plato agrees that rulers should serve the interests of the governed. He accepts rule for the people.

The disagreement

A benevolent ruler cannot know those interests without rule by the people.

Voting reveals the information a decision must take into account: what the people want.

On this view, voting is more than a decision procedure. It is how a benevolent ruler could discover the people’s wants.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 7–8.

Philosophy and facts

Philosophical expertise does not reveal what people want

Logic, metaphysics, ethics, and political philosophy are different from factual information about people’s wants.

“The man who wears the shoe knows best that it pinches.”John Dewey, The Public and Its Problems

An expert shoemaker may still be the best judge of how to fix the problem.

Dewey’s point divides the work: those affected know where the problem lies, while experts may know best how to remedy it.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 8; John Dewey, The Public and Its Problems (1927; Swallow Press, 2016), 223–224.

Plato’s rejoinder

Should rulers track wants or interests?

01Political decisions might aim at people’s interests, meaning what is best for them, rather than what they want.
02If everyone had the same interests, philosophers’ powers of analysis would make them the best judges of those interests.
03In practical terms, people plainly do not share the same interests.

Whatever holds in a deep metaphysical sense, practical politics involves people whose interests differ. The next case makes this concrete.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 8.

Case · the new road

A major road is proposed

01Some people gain if the road is built.
02The owner of a business on the existing main route has the opposite interest.
03People also disagree about the route, since each route benefits different people.

Many competing interests are at stake. Reading philosophy cannot tell a ruler what they are.

Present the facts first, then the lesson: knowing people’s interests requires information from the people affected.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 8.

The case cuts both ways

Competing interests also raise doubts about democracy

01With several routes on offer, no single option may win a majority.
02Even a clear majority choice may be deeply unfair to the minority.
03People judge their interests poorly when short-term and long-term interests diverge.

These problems seem to call for a wise judge who weighs every interest fairly. The need for information supports opinion polling, not yet democracy.

Row 2 recalls the Madisonian concern with protecting minorities; row 3 recalls Hume’s point from the previous chapter. A ruler could gather information through detailed polling without letting the majority decide.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 9.

Case · the student house

Housemates vote on smoking

Housemates disagree about smoking in a shared space they control. They agree to follow a majority vote. But voters may be answering different questions:

Question A

“Would you prefer there to be smoking?”

Question B

“Ought smoking to be permitted?”

Ask students which question they would answer. Question A asks about personal preference; Question B asks a moral question about what the house should allow.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 9–10.

Assessing the vote

Some votes hide the voter’s interests

01Some smokers vote against smoking because it is wrong to impose its effects on others.
02Some non-smokers vote for smoking because they think it is a matter for individual choice.
03These disinterested voters set their preferences aside, so their votes do not reveal their personal interests.

The case undermines the claim that voting reveals what people want. Voters who answer Question B vote on moral grounds, not on preference.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 10.

The consequence

Mixed-motivation voting

Mixed motivation

Some people vote from preference and others from concern for the common good. Voters may not even know their own motives.

The result shows only that more people voted for one option. It reveals neither what the majority wants nor what it thinks is best, and this mess is the normal case today.

This is worse for democracy than the polling point. It is not just that polling may do better; the vote itself may carry no clear information.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 10.

IV

Third line of reply

Voting and the common good

Mixed motivation forces a choice about which motive voters should have.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 10–12.

Two models of voting

Preference voting or common-good voting

Preference voting

Each person votes for what they want.

Weakness: opinion polling gathers finer-grained information about preferences.

Common-good voting

Each person votes for what they think is best for all.

Consequence: the vote shows what the majority thinks is good, not what it wants. It needs a new argument.

The earlier argument said rulers need votes to learn what people want. Common-good voting cannot serve that purpose, so the defense of democracy must change.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 11.

The new argument and its problem

Voting as a way to discover the common good

If people vote on the common good and follow the majority, they may have a very good chance of being right.

This plays back into Plato’s hands. Why expect the rabble to judge better than trained experts, any more than to navigate a ship or make medical decisions?

The objection returns to the craft analogy. A reply needs a reason to think the many can outperform the few.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 11.

Condorcet’s answer

Large majorities of competent voters are reliable

Suppose each voter has a 60% chance of being right. How likely is the majority to be right?

60%
75%
98%
>99.99%
1 voter11 voters101 voters1,001 voters

When each voter is more likely right than wrong, the majority’s chance of being right tends toward certainty as the electorate grows.

The Marquis de Condorcet (1743–1794) gave this mathematical argument. The bar values are an illustrative calculation with each voter independently right 60% of the time; with 10,000 such voters, a majority decision is virtually certain to be right.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 11–12.

Condition 1 · competence

Voters must be more likely right than wrong

Now suppose each voter has only a 40% chance of being right. How likely is the majority to be right?

40%
25%
2%
<0.01%
1 voter11 voters101 voters1,001 voters

The same mathematics now makes a wrong majority almost certain. Condorcet himself doubted that large electorates meet this condition.

The values mirror the previous slide with each voter right 40% of the time. The theorem strengthens whatever tendency individual voters have, for better or worse.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 12.

Condition 2 · motivation

Voters must vote on the common good

The argument assumes every voter answers the same question: what is best for all?

If voters act from particular interests instead, the vote falls back into the confusion of mixed-motivation voting.

Condorcet answers Plato only if both conditions hold. Rousseau’s Social Contract can be read as an account of when they do.

Rousseau published his major writings on democracy about twenty years before Condorcet’s argument, yet he grasped both conditions intuitively. His account will show when democracy can outperform guardianship.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 12.

V

A complementary reply

The values of democracy

Democracy might deserve support even if it were worse at achieving the common good.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 12–14.

Two kinds of justification

Instrumental and intrinsic value

Instrumental

Democracy is good because it achieves something else we value, such as good decisions. Every argument so far has been of this kind.

Intrinsic

Democracy is good in itself, up to a point, even when it fails to produce the best consequences.

The craft analogy judges ruling only by its external results. The intrinsic question asks whether democratic processes have value of their own.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 13.

An analogy

We value some skills for their own sake

A home woodworker’s table costs far more in time than a better, cheaper one from a store. People make tables anyway.

The value of practice

Practicing a craft enriches and tests a person’s physical and mental powers and builds self-worth, whatever the product is worth.

Political participation may have value of this kind, independent of how efficiently it produces good decisions.

The hobby example turns the craft analogy around. Plato treats ruling as a skill valued only for its results; many skills are valued partly for the exercise itself.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 13.

The ship revisited

Not every voyage aims at efficient arrival

01On a training voyage, everyone might take a turn at the helm.
02Even a pleasure-cruise is acceptable if everyone enjoys it and gets home safely.
03Political decision-making may involve values other than reaching a fixed destination.

The same image Plato used against democracy can support it once the purpose of the voyage changes.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, pp. 13–14.

What democratic process expresses

Freedom and equality

Freedom

People have a say in political decisions, especially the decisions that affect them.

Equality

That say is given to all. A further question remains open: who counts as “all”?

These are the two values democracy is most commonly thought to express. The question of who is included returns later in the course.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 14.

Looking ahead

Rousseau’s problem of political order

Find an association that protects each member with the full common force, while each member still obeys only their own will.

“obey only himself, and remain as free as before”Rousseau, The Social Contract, bk. I, ch. 6

Rousseau defends democracy both instrumentally, as a way to reach the common good, and intrinsically, as an expression of freedom and equality.

It is remarkable that Rousseau thinks this problem can be solved at all. The next session examines how.

Citation: “Plato against democracy,” course PDF, p. 14; Rousseau, The Social Contract, bk. I, ch. 6.

Recap

How the case for democracy develops

01Craft analogy: ruling is a skill, so experts should rule. Objections to guardians alone do not defeat this.
02Interests: votes may reveal what people want, but polling does this better and mixed motives blur the result.
03Common good: Condorcet shows majorities can be reliable, but only if voters are competent and vote on the common good.
04Intrinsic value: democracy may matter as an expression of freedom and equality, not only for its results.

Each reply answers the weakness of the one before it. The instrumental and intrinsic defenses together set up Rousseau.

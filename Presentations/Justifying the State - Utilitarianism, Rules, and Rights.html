A collective-action problem

Private calculations create public insecurity

One person breaks a rule

The exception seems to increase happiness.

Everyone claims the same freedom

Possessions remain secure only when each calculation favors the owner.

Trust and production decline

People lose reasons to make things others can simply take.

General misery rises

Individually attractive choices reproduce the prisoner’s dilemma.

Stress the paradox. Each person tries to improve the total from a local point of view, yet the shared practice destroys the security that supports general welfare.

Indirect utilitarianism

The utilitarian repair

Indirect utilitarianism

Introduce the shift from judging each act directly to evaluating the system of rules that structures conduct.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” pp. 3–4.

Act and rule

Rules can maximize happiness indirectly

Direct reasoning

Calculate the expected utility of this act, in this situation, now.

Risk: convenient exceptions erode the system everyone relies on.

Indirect reasoning

Adopt the rules whose general observance produces the best consequences.

Practical direction: obey the law under normal circumstances.

Connect the political argument to the pursuit of personal happiness. A direct effort to become happy can defeat itself, while other aims can produce happiness as a consequence. The same structure supports a stable legal system.

Bentham’s legal program

Utility governs the system of law

01 · ENACTPass laws that outperform every feasible alternative.

Compare each law with competing laws and with having no law.

02 · OBEYTreat law as a standing rule.

Reserve disobedience for preventing disaster.

03 · REVISERepeal laws that fail their utilitarian function.

Political obligation does not protect a harmful legal system forever.

Keep the levels separate. Legislators assess the social effects of rules. Individuals normally obey the resulting laws. A failing law remains open to repeal and replacement.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” p. 4.

The core argument

The utilitarian case for the state

The morally best society maximizes happiness.
The state promotes happiness better than the state of nature.
The state and the state of nature exhaust the feasible alternatives.
Therefore The morally best society has a state.

Add a duty to bring about the best society, and a duty to establish and support the state follows.

The argument remains formally valid if its premises hold. Anarchists can dispute the empirical and comparative premises, but the discussion temporarily grants them in order to test the principle of utility itself.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” pp. 4–5.

Where the argument remains vulnerable

The first premise carries the moral risk

1
The morally best society is the one in which happiness is maximized.

The case for the state can succeed on its own terms while still inheriting every objection to utilitarian morality.

Shift attention from the state-of-nature comparison to the moral foundation of the comparison. The next objection asks what maximizing happiness may permit the state to do.

A real case under pressure

The Birmingham Six

1975 Six men receive murder convictions after an IRA pub bombing.

They maintain that police violence produced false confessions.

1980 Lord Denning treats the institutional consequences of reopening the case as an “appalling vista.”

The reasoning weighs public confidence, cost, and disruption against the prisoners’ claims.

1991 The Court of Appeal quashes the convictions.

Public scrutiny also contributes to improved procedures for recording confessions.

Avoid treating Denning’s judgment as a complete statement of utilitarianism. Use it to show why critics worry about arguments that protect institutional confidence by leaving innocent people in prison.

Citation: Chris Mullin, Error of Judgement, 216, quoted and discussed in “Utilitarianism,” pp. 6–7.

The rule-utilitarian reply

Rights can protect the security utility needs

Standing right No punishment without guilt.
01A system that allows scapegoats makes every person a possible next victim.
02That insecurity depresses happiness across society.
03A stable right against victimization can therefore maximize utility over time.

Present the strongest reply. Rule utilitarianism can support rights because predictable protections create security. The Birmingham Six case may also produce long-term benefits through procedural reform.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” p. 7.

The reply to the reply

Secrecy reopens the objection

secret
victimization

If the public never discovers the practice, fear and insecurity may never enter the calculation.

An efficient hidden injustice could still increase total happiness.

Rule utilitarianism has not yet ruled it out.

Make the conditional structure explicit. Public knowledge explains why open scapegoating lowers utility. Perfect secrecy removes that particular cost, so the rule-utilitarian defense needs another argument.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” pp. 7–8.

A deeper disagreement

The moral reason for rights

Rights as instruments

Protect people because reliable rights usually produce better consequences.

Rights as limits

Protect innocent people whatever benefits their victimization might bring to others.

The disagreement concerns justification, not only policy. Even if utilitarian reasoning usually reaches the correct result, critics argue that it protects the innocent for the wrong reason.

Citation: “Utilitarianism,” p. 8.

Where the argument ends

A strong case for the state rests on a contested moral theory

01Indirect utilitarianism explains why stable laws can outperform act-by-act calculation.
02The state appears to promote happiness better than the state of nature.
03The possibility of justified injustice leaves the principle of utility under pressure.
Unresolved consequence The state may need limits that aggregate happiness alone cannot secure.

A later utilitarian theory of liberty must show whether rights can supply those limits.

Close with the distinction between the argument’s political strength and its moral vulnerability. The next step is to ask whether a utilitarian account of liberty and rights can answer the objection.

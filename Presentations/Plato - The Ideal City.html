Who Should Rule?

Plato’s Ideal City

The just society of the Republic: its three classes, its guardians, and the long education of those who rule.

Political Philosophy

The session builds Plato’s ideal city step by step, in the order the Republic builds it: why a city is needed, who belongs to each class, how the guardians live and are educated, why philosophers must rule, and where justice is found.

Plato calls the finished city Kallipolis, “the beautiful city” (527c).

Citation: Plato, Republic, books II–VII.

Why design a city at all?

The ideal city is built to reveal justice

01The Republic asks what justice is and whether a just person lives better than an unjust one.
02Socrates suggests that justice is easier to see in a city than in one person, like large letters that are easier to read than small ones.
03So he builds a city “in words”: once justice is found there, it can be looked for in the individual soul.

The ideal city is first a thought experiment about justice, and only second a political program. Keep both purposes in view. The challenge comes from Thrasymachus, who claims justice is the advantage of the stronger (338c), and Glaucon, whose ring of Gyges story suggests people are just only because they fear being caught (359c–360d).

Citation: Plato, Republic 338c, 359c–360d, 368c–369a.

I

Part one

How the city begins

The city grows out of human need, and the division of labor shapes it from the start.

Citation: Plato, Republic 369b–376c.

The first city

A city exists because no one is self-sufficient

01Every person needs food, shelter, and clothing, and no one can easily produce all of them alone.
02So people gather together: a farmer, a builder, a weaver, a shoemaker, and later merchants and traders.
03Each works for all and exchanges the surplus. The city is a partnership for meeting needs.

Plato’s starting point is economic, not military or religious: the city exists because cooperation makes everyone better off.

Citation: Plato, Republic 369b–371e.

The founding rule

One person, one job

Principle of specialization

People are born with different natural aptitudes. Each person should do the one task his or her nature suits, because work done by a specialist is done better, more easily, and at the right time.

This principle is the foundation of the whole ideal city. Plato’s eventual definition of justice is this same rule applied to classes: each doing its own work.

Flag this slide as the key to the whole deck. When students later meet the three classes and the definition of justice, they should recognize the principle of specialization at work.

Citation: Plato, Republic 370a–c; compare 433a–b.

From need to war

The healthy city becomes a luxurious city

The healthy city

Citizens live simply on bread, wine, and vegetables. Glaucon mocks it as a “city of pigs.”

The luxurious city

People want couches, delicacies, perfumes, and art. Satisfying these desires requires more land, taken from neighbors.

Wanting more land leads to war. By the principle of specialization, war needs its own trained class: the guardians.

Socrates calls the simple city the true and healthy one, and the luxurious one “feverish.” He agrees to examine the luxurious city because it will show how justice and injustice arise. The ideal city is then a purified version of it.

Citation: Plato, Republic 372a–374e.

What a guardian must be

Guardians must be like well-bred watchdogs

Fierce to enemies

A guardian needs spirit: courage, strength, and quickness to defend the city.

Gentle to friends

A guardian also needs a love of learning, so he or she can tell friend from enemy and treat citizens kindly.

A class with weapons and spirit could easily prey on the citizens it guards. Plato’s answer is that everything depends on education.

Plato jokes that a good dog is “philosophical”: it welcomes the familiar and barks at the stranger, judging by knowledge rather than by treatment received.

Citation: Plato, Republic 375a–376c.

II

Part two

The three classes

Each citizen belongs to the class that fits his or her nature.

Citation: Plato, Republic 412b–417b.

The structure of the city

The ideal city has three classes

RulersComplete guardians

A small group of the wisest guardians. They make decisions for the whole city.

AuxiliariesGuardians as soldiers

Soldiers and officials. They defend the city and enforce the rulers’ decisions.

ProducersThe great majority

Farmers, craftspeople, and traders. They supply the city’s material needs.

“Guardians” is used in two ways: broadly for the whole class of rulers plus auxiliaries, and strictly for the rulers alone, the “complete guardians.” The producers keep private property and families.

Citation: Plato, Republic 414b, 415a–c.

Choosing the rulers

Rulers are tested throughout their lives

01Rulers are chosen from among the guardians: the older and best of them.
02From childhood, guardians face tests of labor, pain, fear, and pleasure, to see whether anything makes them abandon the city’s good.
03Those who keep their conviction that they must always do what is best for the city become rulers. The rest serve as auxiliaries.

Plato compares the tests to testing gold in fire. What qualifies a ruler here is not ambition but reliability: caring for the city as one cares for what one loves.

Citation: Plato, Republic 412c–414b.

The myth of the metals · the story

Every citizen is told a founding myth

Gold

The god mixed gold into those fit to rule.

Silver

Silver went into the auxiliaries.

Iron & bronze

Iron and bronze went into the farmers and craftspeople.

All citizens were born from the same earth, so they are brothers and sisters. Children usually share their parents’ metal, but not always, and each child is placed by his or her own metal.

The myth also contains a warning: an oracle says the city will be destroyed if it is ever guarded by a person of iron or bronze. A gold child born to bronze parents must be raised up; a bronze child born to rulers must be moved down.

Citation: Plato, Republic 414b–415d.

The myth of the metals · its purpose

Why tell a “noble lie”?

To unite the city

If everyone believes they share one mother, the earth, all classes will care for one another and for the city.

To justify each place

Citizens accept their class because it seems to reflect their nature, not the accident of birth or wealth.

Plato admits the story is false and hopes even the rulers will come to believe it. Critics see this as propaganda: the citizens’ agreement is produced by the rulers rather than freely given.

The Greek phrase is often translated “noble lie” or “magnificent myth.” It connects to later objections about consent: can a political order be legitimate if its acceptance rests on a story the rulers know is untrue?

Citation: Plato, Republic 414b–c, 415c–d.

III

Part three

The guardians’ life and early education

Education shapes the souls of those who will protect and rule the city, so it is the city’s most important task.

Citation: Plato, Republic 376c–417b, 423e–424a.

Two halves of early education

Music trains the soul; gymnastics trains the body

Music

Mousikē covers stories, poetry, and music. It shapes a child’s character and love of learning.

Gymnastics

Physical training and a plain diet build the strength and spirit a soldier needs.

Each alone goes wrong. Gymnastics alone makes people savage; music alone makes them soft. Balanced together, they produce the fierce-but-gentle guardian.

Plato insists that even gymnastics is mainly for the soul: its purpose is to strengthen the spirited part, not just the muscles.

Citation: Plato, Republic 376e, 403c–404e, 410b–412a.

Controlling the stories

Poetry is censored for the guardians

01The gods must be shown as wholly good, causing only good things, and never changing shape or deceiving.
02Heroes must not fear death or weep over losses, or young soldiers will learn cowardice.
03Stories must show self-control, not heroes and gods overcome by greed, lust, or fits of laughter.

Children absorb whatever they hear before they can judge it. So the city supervises storytellers, and even much of Homer is struck out.

Plato gives specific examples from Homer and Hesiod, such as Kronos punishing his father and Achilles’ lament. The guiding idea: the soul takes the shape of what it imitates and hears.

Citation: Plato, Republic 377b–392c.

Music and imitation

Art is judged by its effect on character

01Guardians should not act out or imitate base characters, because imitation practiced from youth becomes habit.
02Only musical modes that express courage and moderation are kept; mournful and soft modes are dropped.
03Surrounded by well-ordered art, the young learn to love what is fine before they can explain why it is fine.

This is a claim about moral psychology as much as censorship: good taste is trained early, and reason later recognizes what the well-raised person already loves.

Citation: Plato, Republic 392c–402a.

How the guardians live

Guardians own no private property

01No guardian owns a house, land, or goods beyond basic necessities.
02They live together like soldiers in camp, eat at common meals, and receive a fixed allowance of food from the producers.
03They may not touch gold or silver. They are told they already have divine gold in their souls.

Plato’s reason: once guardians own land and money, they become masters and enemies of the citizens instead of their protectors.

Communism of property applies only to the guardians. The producers keep private property, though extremes of wealth and poverty are also controlled because both corrupt workers (421d–422a).

Citation: Plato, Republic 415d–417b.

An objection and a reply

Are the guardians happy?

The objection

Adeimantus complains that the guardians control the city but enjoy none of its goods: no land, no fine houses, no money.

The reply

The aim is the happiness of the whole city, not of one class. A painter does not color a statue’s eyes purple just because purple is the finest color.

Each class gets the share of happiness that its nature and role allow. Making guardians rich would make them stop being guardians.

The statue analogy: eyes painted purple would no longer look like eyes. Likewise, guardians given wealth would no longer function as guardians. Later, in Book V, Socrates adds that the guardians’ life is in fact the happier one.

Citation: Plato, Republic 419a–421c; 465d–466b.

IV

Part four

Three radical proposals

Plato calls them three “waves” of objection that threaten to drown the argument in ridicule.

Citation: Plato, Republic 449a–474c.

First wave

Women can be guardians and rulers

01Female watchdogs guard and hunt alongside male ones. We do not keep them home to raise pups.
02The only difference that matters is that women bear children. It is as irrelevant to guarding as long hair versus short hair is to shoemaking.
03Natural talents for every job are found in both sexes, so suitable women receive the same education, including gymnastics and war.

This was radical in Athens, where women had no political role. Plato still holds that women are, on average, weaker in every pursuit.

The argument follows directly from the principle of specialization: jobs go by nature, and sex does not determine the relevant nature. Scholars debate whether the proposal reflects concern for women or simply efficient use of talent.

Citation: Plato, Republic 451c–457b.

Second wave · the arrangement

Guardians have no private families

01Among the guardians, there is no private marriage. Wives and children are held in common.
02Rulers arrange mating at festivals with rigged lotteries, so that the best are paired with the best.
03Children are raised in common nurseries. No parent knows his or her own child, and no child knows his or her parents.
04Everyone treats a whole age group as brothers, sisters, parents, or children.

Present the facts first; the next slide explains why. The breeding program also means that those who lose the lottery blame chance, not the rulers. This is one of the most troubling parts of the city for modern readers.

Citation: Plato, Republic 457c–461e.

Second wave · the reason

The city should feel like one body

The greatest evil

Whatever divides a city: people saying “mine” and “not mine” about different things, and rejoicing or grieving separately.

The greatest good

Whatever unites it: when one finger is hurt, the whole body feels the pain. The guardians share every joy and sorrow.

Private families and private property create private loyalties. Removing them removes the strongest source of faction among those who hold power.

Aristotle later objects (Politics II) that what belongs to everyone is cared for by no one: a thousand “sons” get less love than one.

Citation: Plato, Republic 462a–465b.

Third wave

Philosophers must rule

Until philosophers rule as kings, or kings truly philosophize, cities will have no rest from evils.Republic 473c–d

Philosopher

A lover of wisdom who seeks knowledge of what things truly are, not mere opinion about how they appear. Only such a person knows what is actually good for the city.

Plato calls this the biggest wave of all. The distinction between knowledge and opinion (476a–480a) is what makes philosophers fit to rule: opinion grasps changing appearances; knowledge grasps the unchanging Forms.

Citation: Plato, Republic 473c–e, 474b–480a.

What rulers must know

The Good is like the sun

The sun

Makes things visible to the eye and lets living things grow.

The Form of the Good

Makes things understandable to the mind and is the source of their being and value.

The Good is the highest object of knowledge. Someone who has not grasped it cannot reliably judge what is just, beneficial, or good for a city.

Socrates declines to say directly what the Good is and offers the sun as its “offspring” or likeness. The divided line (509d–511e) follows and ranks the ways of knowing.

Citation: Plato, Republic 505a–509b.

The allegory of the cave · the story

Prisoners who mistake shadows for reality

01Prisoners are chained in a cave from birth, facing a wall. They see only shadows cast by objects carried before a fire.
02One prisoner is freed and dragged up into the daylight. Painfully, he learns to see real things, and finally the sun itself.
03When he returns, he stumbles in the dark. The prisoners mock him, and would kill anyone who tried to free them.

Tell the story straight before interpreting it on the next slide. The prisoners even give prizes to whoever is best at predicting the shadows.

Citation: Plato, Republic 514a–517a.

The allegory of the cave · its meaning

The cave shows why the philosopher should rule

Shadows→Ordinary opinion. Most people take appearances and popular beliefs for the truth.
The sun→The Form of the Good. The source of all truth and value.
The freed prisoner→The philosopher. Education turns the soul from appearances toward reality.
The return→Ruling. The philosopher goes back to govern those who still see only shadows.

Plato says education is not putting sight into blind eyes but turning the whole soul toward the light (518b–d). The mockery of the returning prisoner echoes the fate of Socrates.

Citation: Plato, Republic 517a–518d.

The reluctant ruler

Philosophers must be compelled to rule

01Having seen the Good, philosophers would rather keep contemplating it than return to the “cave” of politics.
02But the city educated them for this purpose, so they owe it their service, taking turns at rule.
03This reluctance is a strength: cities are best governed by those who are least eager to rule.

When people who crave power fight over office, they ruin the city. Philosophers have a better life than ruling, so they rule for the city’s sake, not their own.

This is Plato’s answer to “who guards the guardians?”: choose rulers whose deepest desire lies elsewhere, so power offers them nothing they want.

Citation: Plato, Republic 519c–521b.

The rulers’ higher education

Mathematics turns the mind toward what is unchanging

01Arithmetic and geometry force the mind to reason about numbers and shapes that no one can see or touch.
02Solid geometry, astronomy, and harmonics study the perfect patterns behind motion and sound, not the imperfect things we perceive.
03Dialectic, rigorous question-and-answer about what each thing is, is the capstone. It leads up to the Good.

Plato values mathematics not for practical use, though it helps soldiers too, but because it draws the soul from “becoming” to “being.” Dialectic is withheld until age thirty because young people misuse argument for sport.

Citation: Plato, Republic 521c–535a, 537e–539d.

The long road to rule

Fifty years of education come before rule

Childhood

Music and gymnastics

Censored stories, music, and physical training for all guardians.

About 18–20

Military training

Two or three years of compulsory physical and military service.

20–30

Mathematics

The best are selected to study the sciences and see how they connect.

30–35 · 35–50

Dialectic, then service

Five years of dialectic, then fifteen years commanding and holding office “in the cave.”

50 +

Vision and rule

Those who excel see the Good, then alternate philosophy with turns at ruling.

At every stage, some are filtered out. Only those who pass every test, men and women alike, become rulers.

Emphasize the scale: ruling is treated as the most demanding craft of all. Those who fail a stage return to auxiliary roles. Socrates adds that everything said applies to women with the right natures (540c).

Citation: Plato, Republic 536d–540c.

V

Part five

Justice in the ideal city

With the city complete, Plato locates each of its virtues, and then finds the same pattern in the soul.

Citation: Plato, Republic 427e–444a.

The four virtues of the city

Each virtue belongs to part of the city

Wisdom→Found in the rulers, whose knowledge guides the whole city.
Courage→Found in the auxiliaries, who hold firm to what is truly to be feared.
Moderation→Shared by all classes: agreement that the wise should rule.
Justice→Each class does its own work and does not interfere with the others.

Justice turns out to be the principle of specialization from the city’s founding, now applied to classes. It is what makes the other three virtues possible.

Citation: Plato, Republic 428a–434c.

Injustice in the city

Injustice is meddling between classes

01A carpenter doing a shoemaker’s work does little harm.
02But when a producer, made bold by wealth or popularity, tries to become a soldier or a ruler, the city is ruined.

This is why Plato opposes democracy. A system in which anyone may rule, whatever their nature or training, makes this kind of meddling its normal practice.

Link back to the craft analogy in the previous deck: the ideal city is Plato’s positive answer to the question “who should rule?”

Citation: Plato, Republic 434a–c; compare 557a–558c on democracy.

City and soul

The soul has the same three parts as the city

Rulers→Reason, which thinks and should govern the whole person.
Auxiliaries→Spirit, the source of anger, pride, and courage, which should support reason.
Producers→Appetite, the desires for food, drink, sex, and money, which should obey.

A just person is one whose soul is ordered like the ideal city: reason rules, spirit supports it, and appetite is kept in its place.

This answers the question from slide 2. Justice is inner harmony, and the unjust soul is one in civil war. Plato uses this to argue that the just person is happier. Scholars debate how tightly the city-soul analogy is meant to hold.

Citation: Plato, Republic 435b–444a.

Could it exist?

The ideal city is a pattern, not a blueprint

01Plato admits the city may never exist exactly as described. It is a model for judging real cities.
02A city comes closer to it the more closely it follows one condition: philosophers hold power.
03Even if no such city arises, a person can live by its pattern, ordering his or her own soul in the same way.

At the end of Book IX, Socrates says the city is perhaps “a pattern laid up in heaven” for anyone who wishes to see it and found it within himself (592a–b). Interpreters disagree about how seriously Plato meant it as a political proposal.

Citation: Plato, Republic 472b–473b, 592a–b.

Assessing the ideal city

Critics see a city without freedom

01Manipulation: the noble lie secures agreement through a story the rulers know is false.
02Censorship: the rulers control poetry, music, and belief.
03Control of private life: guardians lose their families, and rulers decide who has children.
04No accountability: nothing but their education keeps the rulers from abusing power.

Karl Popper called the ideal city a blueprint for totalitarianism. Defenders reply that Plato aims at everyone’s good, not the rulers’ power.

These objections set up the rest of the unit: expert rule versus democratic accountability. Popper’s critique is in The Open Society and Its Enemies, volume 1 (1945).

Citation: Karl Popper, The Open Society and Its Enemies, vol. 1, 1945.

Recap

The whole city rests on one principle

01The city begins from need, and the principle of specialization gives each person one job fitting his or her nature.
02Applied to the whole city, the principle yields three classes, which the myth of the metals presents as natural.
03Guardians are shaped by censored education and live without property or families, so that nothing divides them from the city’s good.
04Philosophers rule because only they know the Good, and justice means each class, and each part of the soul, doing its own work.

Ask students to trace each later feature of the city back to the principle of specialization.

Citation: Plato, Republic, books II–VII.

Justifying the State

The Social Contract

Can consent turn state power into legitimate authority?

Political Philosophy

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 1–13.

The central problem

Political obligation

The state claims the right to make laws and compel obedience.

What could give one person political power over another?

A benefit can make government useful. Usefulness alone does not give government the right to rule.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 1–2.

Locke’s starting point

Voluntarism

Political power needs a voluntary act

Another person gains political authority over me only when I grant that authority through consent.

This claim concerns political authority, not every moral duty. Duties such as refraining from attacks on innocent people do not wait for consent.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 1.

The contract must satisfy both

Universalism and voluntarism

Universalism

Every adult within the state must fall under its political authority.

Voluntarism

Each obligation must arise through the individual’s own consent.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 2.

The proposed solution

The social contract argument

People begin with a natural right to freedom.
They consent to create or join a political society.
Their consent grants political authority to the state.
The state can then claim a duty of obedience.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 2.
I

Forms of consent

Actual consent

Historical agreement, express agreement, voting, and political participation all try to locate a real voluntary act.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 2–4.

Historical agreement

The original contract

A founding generation may have agreed to leave the state of nature and create civil society.

No historical trace establishes such a moment.
People in the state of nature may lack the concepts needed for a legal agreement.
One generation cannot contract on behalf of every later generation.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 2–3.

Direct agreement

Express consent

Almost no one gives it

Naturalized citizens may pledge allegiance as adults. Most citizens never face an official choice about whether to accept government.

Childhood pledges cannot carry the weight of a voluntary legal commitment.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 3.

The ballot box

Voting as consent

Voting for the government may signal agreement with its authority.
Voting against it may support the system, or may express dissent from the whole system.
Abstaining cannot plausibly communicate consent. Compulsory voting removes the required voluntariness.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 3–4.

A demanding alternative

Participatory democracy

Political obligation may arise only where every citizen takes an active role in government.

Consequence

Contemporary democracies fall short of that ideal, so their citizens lack political obligations on this account.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 4.
II

Consent without words

Tacit consent

Residence and the enjoyment of protection may look like a silent agreement to obey.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 4–6.

Locke’s account

Tacit Consent through residence

“Possession, or Enjoyment” within a government’s territory gives “tacit Consent.”

John Locke, Second Treatise, §119

A person accepts protection or another public benefit.
Accepting that benefit communicates consent.
Consent creates the obligation to obey.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 5; Locke, Second Treatise, §119.

Hume’s objection

Staying does not prove consent

A passenger carried aboard while asleep cannot freely consent when leaving means leaping into the ocean.

David Hume, “Of the Original Contract”

If departure supplies the only possible sign of dissent, remaining has too little meaning to count as a voluntary choice.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 5–6; Hume, “Of the Original Contract.”

Rousseau’s condition

Residence can count only in a free state

Meaningful exit

Leaving must present a genuine and reasonably accessible option.

Constraint

Family, possessions, danger, or the lack of asylum can keep a person in place against their will.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 6; Rousseau, The Social Contract, bk. IV, ch. 2.

The modern condition

The tacit consent verdict

Nation states offer no simple gate through which dissenters can walk.

No free alternative

Another country may refuse admission, and moving may only exchange one objectionable regime for another.

Tacit consent cannot justify the modern state because the conditions for genuine refusal do not exist.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 6.
III

Consent in thought

Hypothetical consent

The contract may describe what rational people would choose in the state of nature.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 7–10.

The thought experiment

The hypothetical contract

Without a state, rational people would freely join together to recreate one.

The imagined choice highlights the state’s value as a source of peace and security.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 7–8.

The voluntarist test

An imagined act creates no obligation

Would consent

A hypothetical choice may show what reason recommends. It does not supply the voluntary act that voluntarism requires.

Hypothetical consent is not consent.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 8.

Two reconstructions

What could hypothetical consent establish?

Worthiness

The state has features that make it worthy of consent, whether anyone actually consents or not.

Disposition

Reflection may reveal a real but previously unexpressed attitude of consent.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 8–9.

First reconstruction

A state worthy of consent

The imagined contract identifies desirable features, especially peace and security.

The basis shifts

The state’s contribution to human well-being now carries the argument. Consent no longer does the justificatory work.

This account approaches a utilitarian defense of the state.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 8.

Second reconstruction

Dispositional consent

A person may possess an attitude of consent without ever expressing or consciously noticing it.

Reflection reveals

Imagining the state of nature may uncover the person’s standing willingness to accept the state.

The argument treats consent like an unspoken belief that becomes conscious only when someone asks the right question.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 9.

The objection

Dispositional consent falls short

Unexpressed willingness rarely creates binding duties in moral or legal life.
Some people reflect and still prefer the state of nature or distrust centralized power.
Calling dissent irrational does not transform dissent into consent.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 9–10.

The structural failure

Voluntarism cannot guarantee universal obligation

A single sincere dissenter breaks the claim that everyone has consented.

Tradeoff

The more seriously a theory takes voluntary choice, the less confidently it can claim authority over every person.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 10.
IV

The consequence

Philosophical anarchism

If no acceptable argument gives the state universal authority, citizens may owe no general duty to obey it.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 10–13.

The anarchist position

Law has no authority merely because it is law

A person should support state action only when independent moral reasons support it.

Fear of punishment may explain obedience without morally justifying it.
Citizens may still follow laws that already match their moral duties.
Citizens may disobey, obstruct, or ignore actions that lack moral authority.
Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 10–11.

The strength

Private judgment sets a moral limit on obedience

Blind obedience would protect laws that persecute minorities or impose death for same-sex relationships.

Critical citizenship

Responsible citizens judge whether a law has moral justification instead of treating legal status as decisive.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, p. 11.

The practical risk

Private judgment can fragment public life

Citizens disagree about taxation, inheritance, and property.

If each person obeys only the laws that match a private moral code, shared rules lose their force.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 11–12.

The challenge to anarchism

Shared public law

Locke’s concern

Conflicting private codes recreate the instability that political society was meant to escape.

The anarchist reply

People might converge on the correct moral principles, but no clear method guarantees that agreement.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 12–13.

The unresolved tension

Autonomy and common rules

Respect for autonomy

Authority needs more than power, usefulness, or inherited membership. Consent captures that demand.

Need for coordination

A society cannot settle every public conflict through separate private judgments.

Consent theory exposes the problem of political authority more successfully than it solves it.

Citation: “The social contract,” attached course PDF, pp. 1–13.

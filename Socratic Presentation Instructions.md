# Socratic Presentation Instructions

This document supplements the presentation authoring standards in `README.md`; it does not replace them. Apply both documents when the user requests a **Socratic presentation**. These instructions add an interactive discussion structure to the existing visual, explanatory presentation style.

## Purpose

A Socratic presentation is a visual Socratic seminar supported by slides. It should do two things in sequence:

1. Explain a philosophical problem, argument, text, or disagreement clearly through purposeful visuals and concise content.
2. Periodically pause to ask the audience a strong leading question grounded in what they have just seen.

The questions should create an opportunity for interpretation, reasoning, and disagreement. They are not quiz questions, rhetorical flourishes, or generic invitations to participate.

The deck should feel like a developing inquiry: explanation establishes a claim or tension, a question asks the audience to think through it, and subsequent slides extend, challenge, or complicate the responses that question is likely to produce.

## Relationship to the existing presentation standards

- Preserve all applicable standards in `README.md`, including projection readability, purposeful visual design, concise section-title slides, and the prohibition on standalone quotation or epigraph slides.
- A Socratic question slide is an intentional discussion pause, not a quasi-title transition slide. It is appropriate to make it a distinct slide when the class needs time to think and speak.
- Do not disguise a section heading as a question. A question slide must pose a genuine, answerable inquiry with meaningful argumentative stakes.
- Do not turn every presentation into this format automatically. Use these instructions when the user asks for a Socratic presentation or clearly requests the seminar-like question structure.

## Plan the inquiry before designing slides

Start with the intellectual arc rather than with a list of slide titles. Identify:

- the central problem or puzzle;
- the major claims, distinctions, examples, and pieces of evidence;
- the points where the audience is likely to need to interpret, compare, challenge, or apply an idea;
- the question the audience should be left with at the end.

Mark natural **hinges** in that arc. A hinge is a point where a coherent unit of explanation has just ended and the audience can productively decide what follows from it. Typical hinges occur after:

- a thinker’s central argument has been reconstructed;
- an important distinction has been made;
- a significant piece of evidence or example has been examined;
- two positions have been compared;
- an objection has exposed a cost, assumption, or unresolved tension;
- the deck is about to move from explanation to application or evaluation.

Before building the deck, it is useful to draft a small question map with these fields: material just covered, discussion question, question type, evidence or concept to use, likely tension in the responses, and the next slide or section that will develop the inquiry.

## Question-slide cadence

As a default, include one discussion question after roughly **three to five substantive content slides**, or at the next meaningful hinge if that comes sooner. Adjust the cadence to the argument:

- Short decks may need two or three question slides.
- Longer decks should usually include one question for each major movement, not a fixed number mechanically applied.
- Place a question immediately after the material it asks the audience to interpret.
- Do not add a question after every slide. Frequent interruptions weaken the explanatory arc and make the questions feel routine.
- Skip a pause when a close reading, demonstration, narrative sequence, or visual comparison needs to remain continuous.
- Include a final question when the goal is synthesis, transfer, or an unresolved judgment rather than simple recap.

Vary the questions’ work across the deck. A useful progression is:

1. **Interpretation:** What is the argument or example actually claiming?
2. **Assumption or diagnosis:** What must be true for the claim to work?
3. **Comparison or application:** How does the idea fare against another view or in a concrete case?
4. **Evaluation or synthesis:** Should we accept the conclusion, and what would accepting it require?

This progression is a guide, not a rigid formula. The question should follow the logic of the material.

## What makes a good leading question

Each question should:

- be based specifically on the preceding slides, text, image, example, or argument;
- be open enough to support more than one defensible response;
- direct attention toward a real philosophical issue rather than a factual recall task;
- require reasons, interpretation, comparison, or evidence;
- expose a tension, assumption, consequence, tradeoff, or unresolved choice;
- be concise enough to read comfortably from the back of a classroom;
- use the key terms the audience has just learned, while remaining understandable without the presenter’s narration;
- invite the audience to engage with the material rather than guess the presenter’s preferred answer.

Prefer questions such as:

- “If the state is justified by protection, what may it legitimately demand in return?”
- “Which assumption about human motivation does this argument need—and is that assumption defensible?”
- “Does this principle treat everyone equally, or does it merely apply the same rule to unequal starting positions?”
- “What would this view require us to give up in the case we have just considered?”

Avoid questions that are merely factual (“What did Locke say?”), answerable with yes or no without further structure, double-barreled, obviously leading to one approved answer, or so broad that the preceding material provides no way to begin.

If a yes-or-no question is useful, add the argumentative task: “Can this account protect liberty without granting the state powers that threaten it? Defend your answer from the argument we just reconstructed.”

## Structure of a question slide

Give each discussion pause a clear visual identity within the deck’s design system. A question slide should normally contain:

- a small, consistent cue such as `Pause`, `Discuss`, or `Question 01`;
- one primary question as the dominant text;
- an optional short anchor naming the relevant thinker, passage, concept, case, or visual;
- an optional brief instruction such as “Use the previous two slides” or “Cite one premise.”

Keep the slide spacious and easy to scan. The question is the content; decorative elements should support the pause rather than compete with it. A relevant diagram, quotation fragment, image, or two-term contrast may be included when it helps the audience think, but do not crowd the prompt with a second lesson or an answer key.

The question slide may be visually quieter or use a deliberate variation in color, scale, or composition so that the audience recognizes a change from explanation to discussion. It should still belong to the same overall visual language as the deck.

Do not include “correct answer,” multiple-choice options, or the presenter’s conclusion on the visible question slide unless the user specifically requests that format. The audience should have to reason rather than select the answer the design has already announced.

## Let the question change what follows

After a question slide, do not immediately present a slide that makes discussion irrelevant. The next section should do at least one of the following:

- supply evidence that tests likely responses;
- introduce an objection or rival interpretation;
- distinguish two answers that initially look similar;
- apply the idea to a new case;
- return to the question with a more precise formulation;
- show what remains unresolved.

When possible, echo the question’s key terms in later slide titles or synthesis language. A question that disappears from the rest of the deck feels ornamental; a question that reshapes the next movement makes the presentation genuinely dialogical.

For the final synthesis, revisit the central question or ask a harder version of it. The ending should leave the audience with a clarified problem, a reasoned comparison, or a judgment they can defend—not only a list of conclusions.

## Speaker notes and facilitation support

Use speaker notes to help the presenter conduct the pause without overcrowding the slide. For each question slide, notes may include:

- what the audience should recall from the preceding slides;
- a suggested pause structure, such as silent writing, pair discussion, whole-class sharing, or a brief text reread;
- the evidence or premises participants should be encouraged to cite;
- one or two neutral follow-up prompts (“What makes you say that?” “Which premise does that rely on?” “Would the same answer hold in the new case?”);
- competing lines of reasoning or likely misconceptions to listen for;
- how the following slides will extend, test, or complicate the discussion.

Notes can prepare the presenter for the intellectual terrain, but should not turn the question into a hidden answer key. The goal is to surface and examine reasoning, not to reward the first response that matches the presenter’s view.

## Visual and accessibility requirements

- Design for classroom projection and distance reading using the existing baseline described in `README.md`.
- Keep the main question large, high-contrast, and comfortably readable without narration.
- Use short line lengths and intentional line breaks; never shrink the question until it is technically contained but difficult to read.
- Preserve sufficient contrast in any special question-slide treatment.
- Use visuals only when they add an interpretive task or clarify a relationship; avoid generic “discussion” imagery.
- Provide meaningful alternative text for images and diagrams.
- Check every question slide for clipping, overflow, awkward wrapping, and accidental ambiguity at the actual presentation size.

## Suggested production workflow

1. Read the existing presentation standards in `README.md` and inspect the current deck library for visual and structural precedents.
2. Define the central problem and the sequence of claims, evidence, distinctions, and objections.
3. Mark the natural hinges where a discussion pause will deepen the inquiry.
4. Draft and test the question map before writing the final slide copy.
5. Build the explanatory slides using the existing presentation standards.
6. Insert question slides at the planned hinges and give them a consistent visual treatment.
7. Add concise facilitation notes and ensure the next slides respond to the questions.
8. Run the normal visual and technical QA, then perform a separate Socratic check using the checklist below.

## Socratic presentation checklist

Before delivering the deck, confirm that:

- The deck is clearly an explanatory presentation with discussion pauses, not a sequence of disconnected prompts.
- The existing presentation standards are still satisfied.
- Question slides appear at meaningful hinges and are not added mechanically after every few slides.
- Every question is grounded in the material immediately preceding it.
- Every question invites reasons, evidence, interpretation, comparison, application, or evaluation.
- The questions are contestable without being vague, and leading without prescribing a single answer.
- The visible slide does not reveal the intended conclusion prematurely.
- The questions are visually distinct enough to signal discussion but consistent with the deck’s theme.
- The following content tests, extends, complicates, or revisits the discussion rather than ignoring it.
- Speaker notes provide practical, neutral facilitation support where useful.
- The final question or synthesis gives the audience a meaningful way to carry the inquiry forward.
- All slides remain legible, accessible, and free of clipping or overflow.

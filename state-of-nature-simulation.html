Building a Society

A state-of-nature simulation on property, authority, and the origins of the state

Six events. Your community must decide, each time, whether to create a new rule, a new office, or a new institution — and whether any of that adds up to a state.
Before we begin

How this works

01

You already wrote an Initial Settlement Plan. Keep it in front of you — every event will pressure-test it.

02

Each event presents a dispute with no clean answer. Decide as a group, then record the decision before moving on.

03

Decisions carry forward. If Event 2 creates a property rule, that rule is now part of your community when Event 4 hits.

04

At the end, you'll fill out a Final Settlement Plan — and answer the boundary question: did you build a state?

1

The private supply

The community discovers that one person secretly removed enough food for five people for one day.

"No one owned the food. I found it and moved it. I am keeping it in case the rest of you make bad decisions."The person who took it
Record your decision before continuing.
Decide
  • Who owns a resource no one has claimed?
  • Who has the authority to judge and punish this person?
2

Competing land claims

Two members claim the same fertile plot beside the stream. Others worry that private ownership will restrict everyone's access to water.

"I marked the land first, so it is mine."First claimant
"I cleared the land and began cultivating it, so it is mine."Second claimant
Decide
  • What creates ownership of land — first possession, or labor?
  • Who has the authority to settle competing claims?
3

The expert who refuses

A member with deep agricultural knowledge believes the community's farming plan will fail. The majority votes for it anyway.

"You may outvote me, but that does not make you correct. I will not help carry out a plan that I believe is dangerous."The expert
Decide
  • Does expertise outweigh the majority's vote?
  • May the community compel the expert to cooperate?
4

The disputed theft

A valuable knife disappears, then turns up among another person's belongings. Friends and relatives begin taking sides.

"I did not steal it. Someone placed it there because they want me punished."The accused

The owner insists the knife's location proves guilt.

Decide
  • Has guilt been established, and by what standard of evidence?
  • Who has the authority to investigate and judge — and must they be neutral?
5

The neighboring settlement

Scouts find another settlement about ten kilometers away — more people, better tools. It has not attacked or threatened anyone.

"If we wait until they attack, it will be too late. We need defenses, guards, and a leader who can act quickly."Pro-defense members
"Preparing for war may convince them we're hostile. Our fear could create the conflict we're trying to prevent."Skeptics
Decide
  • Does the neighboring settlement pose a real threat?
  • How much individual freedom should be traded for collective security?
6

The group that wants to leave

Five members reject the community's rules and want to found a separate settlement. They intend to take a quarter of the food, several tools, seeds they helped collect, personal possessions, and access to part of the stream.

"Losing these resources will endanger everyone who remains."The majority
Decide
  • May members leave, and what may they take with them?
  • Can the majority stop them — and does that make the system truly voluntary?

Debrief · Part 2

Enforcement, legitimacy, and the boundary question

  • Enforcement — What happens when someone refuses a collective decision? Can the community use force — never, only to prevent immediate harm, for limited rules, or for all legitimate decisions?
  • Legitimacy — Why should someone who disagrees accept your community's decisions? Is consent required?
  • The boundary question — At what point, if any, did your community become a state? What feature of your system best supports that answer?
